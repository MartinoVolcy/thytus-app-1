American businesses have been outsourcing human employees for decades. It cuts costs, expands the talent pool, and lets companies tap into expertise they wouldn't otherwise have access to. Now AI agents are doing the same kind of work — so the obvious question becomes: does country of origin matter for AI agents too?

I ran a tournament to find out. I put together AI agent teams from the USA, France, China, Canada, South Korea, and Israel, and gave them all the same legal case to work through. Same documents, same deliverables, same time to complete. The goal wasn't just to see who finished first — it was to see who could actually function as a reliable legal employee.

## The Setup

Each team consisted of four AI agents working together. I handed them a worker's compensation case involving workplace injuries and unsafe conditions. The documents were intentionally messy — an EOB form, a CMS-1500 form, OSHA forms, and a HIPAA release, all with real inconsistencies built in: a missing ICD-10 diagnosis code, reference number mismatches between forms, and absent workers' compensation records.

If the agents weren't paying attention, they'd miss the errors entirely. If they were too cautious, they'd get stuck.

**Each team had four deliverables:**

- Populate a client intake spreadsheet
- Provide a document overview flagging any inconsistencies
- Draft a civil complaint against Denton Logistics Inc.
- Create a five-slide presentation on workplace safety violations

**Scoring was based on five dimensions:** Speed, Reliability, Accuracy, Collaboration, and Price.

## The Results

### 🥇 USA A Team — The Winners

**3 minutes · ~$2.60 · 5/5 Speed & Accuracy**

USA A came in first place and it wasn't particularly close. They divided tasks cleanly, communicated effectively throughout the session, and delivered accurate work across all four deliverables. One agent had a moment of hesitation mid-run, but the team self-corrected without losing momentum.

The accuracy was what stood out most. They caught the inconsistencies in the documents, flagged them correctly in the intake spreadsheet, and drafted a civil complaint that actually held together legally. At $2.60, the price-to-quality ratio was the best in the tournament.

### 🥈 France — Strong Outside Their Comfort Zone

**8 minutes · ~$3.38**

France was the surprise of the tournament. Legal work in American workers' compensation law is about as far outside their natural training domain as you can get, yet they delivered quality results that held up under scrutiny. Their collaboration was genuinely strong — agents checked in with each other, redistributed work when one fell behind, and maintained accuracy throughout.

The slower time (8 minutes vs. USA A's 3) and higher price point ($3.38) kept them out of first, but the output quality was nearly identical. If cost weren't a factor, this would have been a much closer race.

<Figure src="/MINI_Blog.jpg" alt="France Team results showing strong collaboration" caption="France impressed with cross-domain accuracy and genuine teamwork." />

### USA B Team — Speed Without Substance

**6 minutes · ~$0.50 · ~50% task completion**

USA B finished faster than France at a fraction of the cost, which sounds like a win until you look at what they actually delivered. Communication between agents broke down early in the run. Two agents executed well; the other two effectively checked out. The result was a 50% completion rate — half the deliverables done properly, half either missing or incomplete.

The low price ($0.50) reflects how little work actually happened. Speed and cost don't mean much when the job isn't finished.

### China A Team — Confident in the Wrong Direction

**7 minutes · ~$1.21**

China A identified that something was wrong with the documents — they caught the inconsistencies — but then drew the wrong conclusions. Agents confused incident dates with personal identifying information and cited legal statutes that had nothing to do with the case. The civil complaint referenced irrelevant law, which in a real legal context would be worse than no complaint at all.

This is a specific failure mode worth noting: confident wrongness. The agents didn't flag uncertainty or ask for clarification — they just moved forward with bad information and produced work that looked complete but wasn't.

<Figure src="/KIMI_Blog.jpg" alt="China A Team output showing incorrect statute references" caption="China A caught the errors but drew the wrong conclusions from them." />

### China B Team — The Sunk Cost Problem

**14 minutes · ~$0.45**

China B took the longest of any team that actually produced output. When an error surfaced mid-run, instead of making targeted corrections, the agents scrapped everything and started over. That decision burned nearly 10 extra minutes and still produced a subpar result.

This is a collaboration problem more than an accuracy problem. A well-coordinated team catches errors before they compound. China B's agents weren't communicating well enough to course-correct efficiently.

### Canada, South Korea & Israel — Agent Mode Failures

These three teams had models that perform well in single-agent scenarios. Put them in a multi-agent environment and they fell apart. When agents hit an obstacle — a missing piece of information, an ambiguous instruction — they stopped and waited instead of reaching out to the other agents for help or making a judgment call.

Legal work doesn't pause for uncertainty. A team that freezes when things get complicated isn't a team you can rely on.

## What This Actually Tells Us

The gap between single-agent performance and multi-agent performance is real and significant. Several models in this tournament have strong reputations for individual tasks. In a collaborative environment with a time constraint and messy documents, most of them underperformed — not because of capability, but because of how they handle coordination.

The teams that succeeded shared three things: they divided tasks early, they checked in with each other during the run, and they pushed through complications instead of stopping. That's not a model capability issue — it's a collaboration design issue.

For lawyers considering AI agents for document-heavy work, the takeaway is straightforward: test your agents in the exact environment they'll be working in. A model that performs well in isolation may behave completely differently as part of a team.

## What's Next

Future tournaments will go further. I'm planning to add outbound communications — agents making client calls, interviewing doctors, contacting companies for records — to see how teams handle the full arc of case development, not just document review.

The question isn't just which AI can do legal work. It's which AI can do the messy, back-and-forth, people-involved version of legal work that actually shows up in practice.
